Science Fiction is a Mirror

I don’t think science fiction is mainly about the future. I think it’s about the present — just with enough distance that you can finally see the shape of it.

The best sci-fi doesn’t really predict. It takes one thread from today — surveillance, comfort, ambition, politics, language — and keeps pulling until something breaks. That’s what makes it useful to me. It’s entertainment, sure, but it’s also a way of thinking.

Take utopia. Wall-E gives us a world where every human need is met. No friction, no pain, no inconvenience. Sounds great right up until you look at what that world does to the people living in it. They don’t flourish. They kind of soften into uselessness.

That’s the problem with first-order thinking. More comfort, more intelligence, more convenience — all of that sounds obviously good. But if everyone reaches “peak,” peak just becomes the new average. The baseline moves. What still matters then is whatever remains rare: agency, taste, creativity, character.

Pull the comfort thread a little harder and you get Brave New World. No one is really oppressed. They’re sedated. Everyone is pleasant, productive, and fundamentally incurious. There’s no rebellion because there’s barely any reason to rebel when the system keeps you vaguely satisfied all the time.

Maslow would tell you that once basic needs are met, people move toward belonging and self-actualization. But what if the system keeps you parked at comfortable? What if it removes just enough friction that you never have to seriously ask what you want, what matters, or what you’re here for?

That’s what makes that kind of world unsettling. The danger isn’t pain. It’s comfort that is just good enough to keep you from becoming more.

Then there’s the language problem. 1984 gave us words like Doublethink, Newspeak, and the Ministry of Truth. We usually read that as a warning about governments, but honestly, it also describes a lot of corporate behavior with uncomfortable precision.

The Ministry of Truth shows up anywhere leaders would rather rewrite the narrative than admit the strategy was wrong. Anywhere being seen as right matters more than actually being right. Orwell’s real insight wasn’t just political. It was about power, and about the language people use to protect it.

Ender’s Game does something similar with strategy. On the surface it’s about tactical brilliance. Underneath, it’s about manipulation, incentives, and the machinery around conflict. The battle is almost never the whole story. The system producing the battle is the story.

That’s why I like science fiction so much. It gives you compressed simulations for thinking about the world. A policy playing out across centuries. A technology reshaping a civilization. A language game slowly corrupting truth.

I read sci-fi for pattern recognition. Not so I can know what’s coming, but so I can get better at asking “and then what?” about what’s already here.
